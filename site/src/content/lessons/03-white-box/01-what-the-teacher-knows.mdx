In Module 2 the teacher gave one answer per message. That answer is a small part of what it knows. This module is about getting the rest.

## One label throws information away

Take the message *"I still haven't got my new card."* A person labels it `card_arrival`. As a training target, that label says: this type is right, and the other 76 are all equally wrong.

They are not equally wrong. A question about when a card will be delivered is nearly the same request. A lost card is related. A question about exchange rates has nothing to do with it. Someone who knows the task knows this, and the label does not say it.

A teacher model does say it, if you look at its probabilities instead of its top answer.

Geoffrey Hinton called this <mark>dark knowledge</mark>: what a model knows about how the wrong answers relate to the right one. A student trained on these probabilities learns something from every type on every example, not from one type.

## Why this helps most with few labels

  options={["About the same: one fact", "Something about every one of the 77 types", "Less, because probabilities are noisier than labels"]}
>
  Something about every type. The probabilities tell the student which types are close to this message and which are far away, all at once. This is why a student can learn more from a few thousand teacher-scored messages than the count alone suggests, and why the effect is strongest when human labels are scarce.
</PredictReveal>

It matters most where types are easy to confuse. In Module 2 you saw that the weak spots on Banking77 are pairs of near-identical types. A plain label gives a student no hint that two types are neighbors. The teacher's probabilities show it on every example where the two compete.

## White-box distillation

<mark>White-box distillation</mark> trains the student to match the teacher's probabilities, not just its answers. It is called white-box because you have to see inside the teacher: an API that returns text does not show you these numbers, so you need a model whose weights you can run yourself.

The rest of this module covers the three things you need:

1. How to read a probability for every type out of a language model.
2. Temperature, a setting that controls how much of the teacher's uncertainty the student sees.
3. The loss that pulls the student toward the teacher's probabilities and the human labels at once.

Then you will run it and measure how much of the gap from Module 2 it closes.

<ReviewCard
  client:load
  fromModule="Baselines and evaluation"
  question="What does “gap closed” measure?"
>
  The share of the distance from the baseline to the teacher that the distilled student covered: (distilled − baseline) ÷ (teacher − baseline).
</ReviewCard>
