"Fast" means two different things for a service, and they pull against each other.

## Latency and throughput

- <mark>Latency</mark> is how long one request takes, from sending it to getting the answer. A person waiting for a reply cares about this.
- <mark>Throughput</mark> is how many messages the service handles per second. A job processing a million messages overnight cares about this.

Latency is reported as percentiles, not an average. The **p50** is the time half of requests beat. The **p95** is the time 95% of them beat. The slow tail is what users notice and what an average hides: a service averaging 20 milliseconds can still make one caller in twenty wait a second.

## Batching

A GPU processes 32 messages together in little more time than it takes to process one. Sending messages in batches therefore raises throughput a great deal.

| A nightly job over stored messages | Throughput, and so cost | Large batches |
| Both, on the same service | Both | Let callers choose their batch size; that is what the course's service does |

## From throughput to cost

A self-hosted model costs the price of its machine by the hour, whatever it is doing. Its cost per message is that hourly price divided by how many messages it handled in the hour.

Two cautions about that cost figure.

**It assumes the machine is busy.** A machine that could handle 2,000 messages a second and receives 20 costs a hundred times more per message than the calculation says. For a self-hosted model the real cost depends on how full you keep it. This is the break-even point from lesson 1.1.

**It leaves out people.** Someone has to keep the service running, retrain the student when the task shifts, and answer for it when it is wrong. An API bundles that into its price.

## Comparing with the teacher fairly

To compare the student with the teacher, measure both the same way: the same messages, the same hardware, each at a batch size that suits it. Report accuracy next to cost, never cost alone. A model that is ten times cheaper and ten points worse may or may not be a good trade, and that judgment belongs to whoever owns the product.
