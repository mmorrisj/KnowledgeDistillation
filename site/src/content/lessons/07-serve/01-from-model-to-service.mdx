A student sitting in a notebook has saved nobody any money. The point of distilling was to replace calls to a large model with calls to a small one, and that only happens when the small one is running somewhere other software can reach it. This module covers that last stretch.

## What "serving" needs

- **A fixed artifact.** One folder holding the weights, the tokenizer and the label names, so that what you tested is exactly what runs.
- **A service.** A small program that loads the artifact once and answers requests over the network.
- **Limits and checks.** What size of request it accepts, what it does with a bad one, and how you know it is alive.
- **Measurements.** How long a request takes, how many it can handle, and what each costs.

None of this is specific to distillation. It is ordinary software work, and it is the part most often skipped by people who came to the problem through modelling.

## Which student?

Over the course you built several students for the banking task: small encoders, and text-generation models with and without LoRA. They do the same job at very different running costs.

  The encoder. It produces all 77 scores in a single pass, while a generative student writes its answer one token at a time through a model ten times the size. When quality is the same, the cheaper model wins, because the running cost is paid on every request for as long as the service lives. Keep generative students for tasks whose output is text.
</PredictReveal>
