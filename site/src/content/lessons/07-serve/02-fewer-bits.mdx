Distillation made the model smaller by giving it fewer weights. <mark>Quantization</mark> makes it smaller again by storing each weight less precisely. You met it in Module 5 as a way to fit a large model on a card for training. Here it is used for serving.

## Rounding to a short list of values

A weight stored in 32 bits can take billions of different values. Stored in 8 bits it can take 256. Quantizing means choosing those 256 values to cover the range the weights actually span, and rounding every weight to the nearest one.

<QuantizationExplorer client:load />

Drag the slider down. At 8 bits the rounded weights sit almost exactly on the originals. At 4 bits you can see them move. At 2 bits several different weights collapse onto the same value.

## What you gain and what you pay

<div class="table-wrap">

| Bits per weight | Size against 32-bit | Typical effect on accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| 16 | Half | None you can measure |
| 8 | A quarter | Usually very small |
| 4 | An eighth | Small to moderate; depends on the model and task |

</div>

Size falls in proportion to the bits. Speed is less predictable. On a CPU, 8-bit arithmetic is usually clearly faster. On a GPU, 4-bit weights are often slower, because they have to be unpacked before each use; there the gain is memory, not speed.

  Too small to tell. On 1,000 messages the margin is well over two points, and a paired comparison of the two models will almost certainly include zero. That is the common result at 8 bits, and it is good news: a quarter of the size for no measurable loss. Had the drop been five points, the same comparison would have told you it was real.

## Always measure

Quantization changes the model, so the test score you had no longer applies. Score the quantized model on the same test set and compare the two with a paired test, exactly as you compared students in earlier modules. It takes a minute and replaces a guess with a number.

```python
from kd_course.serving.export import ClassifierPredictor, model_size_mb, quantize_int8

small = quantize_int8(student.model)                              # 1
print(model_size_mb(student.model), model_size_mb(small))         # 2

before = ClassifierPredictor(student.model, tokenizer, 64).predict(test_texts)
after = ClassifierPredictor(small, tokenizer, 64).predict(test_texts)   # 3
```

</WorkedExample>

The method used here is the simplest kind: the weights are rounded after training, with nothing else changed. It runs on a CPU, which suits a small encoder well. For large generative models on a GPU, 4-bit loading as in Module 5 is the usual choice.

## The order of the steps

If the student was trained with LoRA, recall the rule from lesson 5.4: merge the adapter into full-precision weights first, and quantize the merged model afterwards. Quantizing first and merging second rounds the result twice.
