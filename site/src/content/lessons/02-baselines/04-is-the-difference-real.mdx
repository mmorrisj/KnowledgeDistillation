## A score is an estimate

A test set is a sample of all the messages the model might ever see. Draw a different thousand messages and the score would come out a little different, even though the model has not changed. The score you measured is an estimate of the model's true quality, and it has a margin of error.

A <mark>confidence interval</mark> states that margin. "72.4%, with a 95% interval of 69.6% to 75.1%" means the true value is very likely somewhere in that range.

Two things set how wide the interval is: how many test messages you have, and how close the score is to 50%. Only the first is under your control.

Move the test-set slider and watch the verdict change. With the starting numbers, a 3-point gap on 500 messages cannot be told apart from luck. The same gap on a few thousand messages can.

A figure worth remembering: on 1,000 test messages, a score near 75% has a margin of about 2.7 points either way. A gain smaller than that needs a closer look before you believe it.

## How the interval is calculated

The course uses the <mark>bootstrap</mark>, which needs no formulas:

1. From your 1,000 test results, draw 1,000 at random, allowing repeats.
2. Score that resampled set.
3. Repeat a thousand times.
4. Take the range that holds the middle 95% of those scores.

It works for any metric, including macro-F1, where there is no simple formula.

## Comparing two models fairly

When two models are scored on the same test messages, there is a sharper comparison than checking whether their intervals overlap. Some messages are hard for every model and some are easy. A <mark>paired</mark> comparison resamples the messages once and scores both models on the same resample, so the difficulty of the sample cancels out. The result is an interval for the difference itself.

If that interval lies entirely above zero, the second model is better. If it includes zero, you cannot say.

## The other source of luck

Training has randomness too: the order of the examples and the starting values of new layers. Train the same model twice with different random seeds and the scores can differ by a point or more, especially with few training examples. When a decision rests on a small difference, train with three seeds and look at the spread.

## How to report a result

Give the reader what they need to judge it. The numbers in this example are made up; the form is what matters.

> Baseline 61.3% (95% interval 58.2 to 64.3). Distilled 68.9% (66.0 to 71.7). Difference +7.6 points (4.9 to 10.4), paired, on 1,000 test messages.
