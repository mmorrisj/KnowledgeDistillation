This lesson covers two ideas you will meet as soon as you read about distilling writers. Neither is needed to run the lab. Both explain why the standard method sometimes falls short, and what people do about it.

## A student that cannot do everything

A teacher with billions of weights can keep several quite different good answers in mind for one input. A student with a fraction of the capacity may not be able to represent all of them. When it cannot, it has to compromise, and the loss you choose decides how.

The teacher term in the distillation loss uses KL divergence, a measure of how far one distribution is from another. It is not symmetric. Measuring from the teacher's side and from the student's side give different answers, and push the student toward different compromises.

## Forward KL: cover everything

<mark>Forward KL</mark> is large wherever the teacher has probability and the student does not. To keep it small, the student must put some weight on everything the teacher might say. A student that cannot make two separate peaks spreads itself across both, and ends up putting weight on the space between them too, where the teacher has almost none.

For a writer, that in-between space is output the teacher would never produce: a title that blends two different readings of the message. Forward KL is what the loss in lesson 6.3 uses, and it is the default almost everywhere.

## Reverse KL: commit to something

<mark>Reverse KL</mark> is large wherever the student has probability and the teacher does not. To keep it small, everything the student produces must be something the teacher approves of. A limited student picks one of the teacher's peaks and stays there. It misses some of what the teacher can do, and what it does produce is sound.

## Why not always use the reverse direction

Reverse KL is measured on what the student produces, so training needs text generated by the student itself, scored by the teacher, in a loop. Both models are busy throughout training, which is slower and harder to set up than reading saved teacher records. On a narrow task with consistent teacher outputs, forward KL usually does well enough. Reach for the reverse direction when a forward-trained student writes blended or off-target text.

## Trained on the teacher's words, used on its own

There is a second mismatch, between training and use.

During training the student always continues from the teacher's text: given "New card not", predict the next token. It never sees its own mistakes. During use it continues from its own earlier tokens. If it writes a slightly unusual second word, it is now in a situation training never showed it, and the next choice is more likely to go wrong as well. This is often called <mark>exposure bias</mark>.

The remedy is related to the one above: let the student write, have the teacher say what it would have written next from the student's own words, and train on that. It is the same extra machinery, for the same price.

For short outputs such as an eight-word title, the effect is small: there is little room to drift. For long outputs it grows, and it is one reason long-form generation is harder to distill well.

## What the lab does

The lab uses forward KL on saved teacher records, which is the practical default. You now know the two limits of that default and the name of what comes next if you hit them.
