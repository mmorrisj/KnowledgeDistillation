In Module 3 the student matched the teacher's probabilities over 77 types. <mark>Token-level distillation</mark> is the same idea with one change: the probabilities are over the next token, and the matching happens at every position of the answer.

## One position

Suppose the teacher has written "New card not" and is choosing the next token.

The title it finally wrote says "received". Sequence-level distillation teaches the student that one word. The probabilities also say that "arrived" and "delivered" would have been fine and "working" would have been wrong. That is the dark knowledge of Module 3, now available at every token.

## Reading the probabilities along an answer

You do not ask the teacher to generate again. You show it the prompt followed by the title it already wrote, in one pass, and read off its next-token distribution at each position of the title. This is sometimes called teacher forcing: the teacher is held to its own earlier words while you record what it thought at each step.

## The storage problem

<TopKExplorer client:load />

The standard fix is to keep the <mark>top-k</mark>: the teacher's k most likely tokens at each position, with their probabilities, and nothing else. The kept probabilities are rescaled to add up to 100%. A k of 20 is a common starting point.

Keeping too few loses the alternatives you wanted the student to see. Keeping too many stores noise. As with other settings, try two values and compare on validation data.

## The loss

At each position of the answer, the loss is the one from lesson 3.4:

> loss = alpha × teacher term + (1 − alpha) × token term

- **The teacher term** pulls the student's probabilities for the kept tokens toward the teacher's, with a temperature and the T² correction.
- **The token term** is ordinary cross-entropy against the token the teacher actually wrote. This is the sequence-level loss.

The total is averaged over every position of every answer. With alpha at 0 you are back to sequence-level distillation exactly.


## Why the tokenizer must match

The teacher's record for a title is a list of token numbers. The student is asked to give those same token numbers high probability. If the two models split text differently, token 5,012 means one thing to the teacher and something else to the student, and the record is meaningless. Models in one family share a tokenizer, so a 7B teacher and a 0.5B student from the same family line up exactly.

Sequence-level distillation has no such limit, because text can be re-split by any tokenizer.
