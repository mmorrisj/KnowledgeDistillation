This lab distills the 7B teacher's title writing into the 0.5B student, two ways. It does not need results from earlier labs.

## What the lab does

1. **The teacher writes titles** for 2,000 pool messages, and for 300 test messages it will not train on.
2. **Titles that break the rules are dropped** from the training set.
3. **The teacher's top-20 tokens are recorded** at every position of every training title.
4. **Baseline:** the untrained student writes titles for the test messages, given the teacher's full instructions.
5. **Sequence-level student:** trained on the teacher's titles.
6. **Token-level student:** trained on the teacher's token probabilities as well.
7. **Scoring:** rules and overlap for every model, then the teacher is loaded again to judge them all.

The students here are fully fine-tuned, because the 0.5B model fits comfortably. Everything in the lab works unchanged with the LoRA adapters from Module 5 if you move to a larger student.

## Before you look at the results

Predict and write down:

- How often will the untrained student stay within eight words? How often will the trained ones?
- Will token-level distillation beat sequence-level? By enough to see?
- What share of the teacher's own titles will the judge accept?

## Reading the results

The lab prints one table with four rows: the teacher, the untrained student, and the two distilled students.

- **Within the word limit.** Expect the trained students to do at least as well as the teacher here, because rule-breaking titles were filtered out of their training data. This is lesson 6.2's point, measured.
- **Overlap with the teacher.** The teacher's row is 100% by definition. A higher figure for a student means closer imitation, not necessarily better titles.
- **Judge acceptance.** Remember the judge is the teacher. Read the teacher's own acceptance rate as the ceiling for this judge, and pay most attention to the gaps between students.
- **The paired comparisons.** One tells you whether training beat the untrained baseline. The other tells you whether token-level beat sequence-level.

A small or unclear gain from token-level over sequence-level is a common result on a short, narrow task, where the teacher's titles already carry most of what there is to learn. It mirrors the top-answers against probabilities comparison from Lab 3. The token-level signal tends to matter more as outputs get longer and the student gets smaller.

Finally, the lab prints ten messages with all four titles side by side. Read them before trusting the table.

## If something goes wrong

<div class="table-wrap">

| What you see | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Out of memory when the teacher loads the second time | A student is still on the GPU | Run the cell that frees the student, then load the teacher again |
| The untrained student's titles are long paragraphs | Expected: an untrained small model follows instructions loosely | Nothing. This is the baseline's weakness, and the table will show it |
| Many training titles dropped by the rules | The teacher is ignoring the word limit | Print a few raw replies; consider tightening the teacher's prompt |
| The judge's verdicts are mostly "no answer" | The judge is not replying with yes or no | Print a few raw judge replies and compare with the question it was asked |
| Token-level training loss is not falling | The learning rate was changed | Use about 2e-5 for full fine-tuning |

</div>
